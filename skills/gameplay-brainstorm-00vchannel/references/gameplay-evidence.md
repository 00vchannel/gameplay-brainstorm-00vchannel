# Gameplay evidence and beginner playtesting

Read this reference only when a task needs a playtest method, an experience measure, or a theory lens.

## Evidence states

Attach an evidence state to one specific hypothesis, not to the entire game.

- **Untested:** supported only by design rationale, theory, another game's precedent, or an unrun prototype.
- **Directional support:** limited or formative player evidence agrees with the hypothesis, but other explanations remain plausible.
- **Supported:** appropriate target players, methods, and repeated evidence support the hypothesis within a stated scope.
- **Disconfirmed:** a predefined failure signal appears, or observations conflict with the predicted causal chain.

One supported hypothesis does not establish that the whole game is fun or validated.

## Minimum playtest design

Define these before testing:

1. The design decision the test must inform.
2. A falsifiable prediction about player behavior, understanding, or experience.
3. The target participants and relevant prior experience.
4. The rules, content, task, session context, moderator behavior, and any comparison condition.
5. The observable behavior or telemetry that answers the research question.
6. Non-leading interview prompts about what players did, expected, noticed, and felt.
7. Support, failure, and stop signals justified by a baseline, comparison, or product requirement.

Use early formative tests to find problems and choose the next design change, not to claim population-wide effects. Derive participant counts and analysis from the research question, baseline, stakes, and required confidence. Never apply a universal participant count. When the required information is unavailable, use `TBD` and state what evidence is needed to decide.

## Non-leading observation and interview guidance

- Observe choices, hesitation, errors, recovery, attention, and changes in strategy before explaining the intended design.
- Ask what the player believed was happening and what they expected an action to cause.
- Ask for concrete moments rather than agreement with labels such as "fun," "clear," or "strategic."
- Separate what the player said from the designer's interpretation of why it happened.
- Treat a stated preference as evidence of preference, not automatic evidence of future behavior.

## Optional theory lenses

Use a lens only when it changes the design question:

- **Rules to experience:** what behavior do the rules actually encourage?
- **Meaningful choice:** are options understandable, consequential, and useful in different situations?
- **Learning and competence:** can players connect results to actions they can understand and improve?
- **Social connection:** use only when cooperation, competition, or shared memories are intended experiences.
- **Challenge and attention:** where does difficulty come from, and does feedback support learning?
- **Uncertainty:** distinguish execution, information, opponents, randomness, complexity, progression, and narrative uncertainty.

Theory and comparable games can strengthen a hypothesis; they cannot replace player evidence.

## Questionnaires and scales

When using a validated questionnaire or scale, preserve its official items, order, scoring, and validated language. If any of these change, label the result as an adapted or custom instrument and do not compare it directly with official benchmarks without appropriate validation.
